Who We Are

Interfaith Sanctuary is a gathering place, not a doctrine. We were not built to replace the church, synagogue, mosque, temple, or grove anyone already calls home — we were built for the ground between them, where a Jew, a Christian, a Muslim, a Hindu, a Buddhist, an animist, and the simply searching can sit in the same pews and mean the same prayer in different words.

What holds us together isn't a shared belief about God. It's a shared agreement about how to treat one another while we each work that question out for ourselves.

Our Approach

Human Beings First

We don't gather here to agree on who God is. We gather to meet each other as human beings first — and to let the healing that happens between two people move outward, the way it always has.

One person heals. That healing reaches their family. Families steady their communities. And communities, over time, are what change a society. It is slow, unglamorous work — but it is the only kind that lasts.

How We Keep This Space Safe

Sanctuary Ground Rules

A sanctuary held for everyone still needs a floor to stand on. These are ours.

◈

No Gurus

No one here speaks for the Divine on anyone else's behalf. Everyone is their own guru — we can guide, but we don't decide for you.

◈

No Trolling, Ghosting, or Silent Treatment

Disagreement is welcome. Cruelty and disappearing on people are not. Persistent bad faith is grounds for exile from the circle.

On the Shadow We Share

Recommended Reading: The Study of Evil

Every tradition that gathers people also has to name what pulls people apart — selfishness, cruelty, the slow talking-of-oneself into harming others. Each names it differently, and we don't ask anyone here to trade their word for someone else's. So the sanctuary's reading list doesn't circle around comfort. It circles around evil itself — how it forms, how it hides, and how it's recognized — because a sanctuary that can't look at that honestly isn't much of a sanctuary.

Carl Jung spent much of his life on this same problem, and his conclusion was blunt: a person doesn't grow toward the light by picturing themselves as good. They grow by making their own darkness conscious. Left unexamined, he warned, that darkness doesn't go away — it quietly takes over, directing a life from underneath, until what was never faced gets mistaken for fate.

— after C.G. Jung, on the shadow

Recommended Reading
Malignant Self-Love
Sam Vaknin — Narcissism Revisited

A clinical look at narcissism from someone who has lived it and studied it. Useful for naming a pattern of harm without needing to diagnose the person in front of you.

Recommended Reading
Twelve Steps to a Compassionate Life
Karen Armstrong

A religious historian distills every major tradition to one shared denominator: active, practical empathy. It moves past “who is right” entirely and becomes a hands-on guidebook for training ourselves to show up for one another in daily life — the practice that answers the shadow the rest of this shelf names.
